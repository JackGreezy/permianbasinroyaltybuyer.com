Sell Mineral Rights in Pecos County, TX/NM

Pecos County runs from the ranch country south of Fort Stockton into some of the most drilled acreage on the western Delaware shelf, and what your interest is worth depends entirely on which side of the county line you're standing on.

the buyer team spent enough years on drilling rigs west of Fort Stockton to know Pecos County doesn't behave like one basin. The western half is still mostly Ellenburger and Devonian vertical production, some of it dating back to the 1950s, held by families who've cashed the same small check every month for two generations. The eastern half, up against the Reeves County line, is a different animal entirely — stacked Wolfcamp and Bone Spring benches, multi-well horizontal pads, and operators who weren't drilling there ten years ago.

If you inherited a fraction here, or your grandfather leased 640 acres south of the buyer team-10 back when Fort Stockton was still mostly sheep and cattle, the fair value of that interest hinges on whether you're sitting under active development or legacy vertical wells that peaked decades ago. the buyer team looks at both the same way — pull the runsheet, check who's operating nearby, and tell you honestly which bucket you're in before the buyer team quotes a number.

What a royalty check from Pecos County actually looks like

If you're getting a statement from an operator on eastern Pecos County acreage, expect it to move — sometimes sharply — with new wells coming online in the unit, then decline steadily as those wells mature. That volatility is exactly why some owners choose to sell: locking in a value now avoids riding the decline curve down over the next several years, especially if you don't need the monthly income and would rather have the lump sum.

On the western, older-vertical side of the county, checks tend to be smaller and steadier, and a sale there is usually less about timing the market and more about simplifying an estate or converting a tiny, easy-to-lose-track-of interest into cash you can actually use.

Fort Stockton, Sheffield, and the county's two personalities

Fort Stockton is the county seat and where you'll find the courthouse records, but it's also roughly the dividing line for the geology that matters to your check. West and south of town, toward the Glass Mountains and the Trans-Pecos ranch country, most of the production is older, shallower, and declining the way you'd expect a 40-year-old vertical well to decline — slow, low-volume, predictable.

East toward Sheffield and the Reeves County boundary, Apache and other operators have been running horizontal Wolfcamp and Bone Spring laterals through stacked pay for the better part of a decade. That's the part of the county where a small legacy interest can suddenly be worth multiples of what it was five years ago, simply because a new unit got permitted across it. Knowing which side of that line your tract sits on is the first thing the buyer team checks, not the last.

University and state land neighbors

Pecos County sits close enough to University Lands' Permian holdings that a lot of the operators active on private minerals here are the same names running rigs on state and university acreage a county or two over. That matters because it means the drilling pace on your section can be driven by an operator's broader development plan rather than anything specific to your tract — a good reason to check current permitting before assuming your interest is dormant just because nothing's happened on it in a while.

It also means split estate is common. A lot of the ranching families out here sold the surface generations ago but kept the minerals, or the reverse, and title can carry surface-use agreements, old pooling orders, or non-participating royalty carve-outs that a buyer needs to read carefully before quoting anything.

Working from a Pecos County courthouse title

The county clerk's office in Fort Stockton holds deed and probate records going back to the county's earliest oil days, and because so much of this land changed hands through ranching-family inheritance rather than clean corporate sale, a lot of the mineral title out here runs through wills, heirship affidavits, and undivided fractional interests split among cousins who may not all agree on what to do.

That's normal, not a dealbreaker. the buyer team has bought fractional interests where four heirs wanted to sell and one didn't — you can typically sell just your undivided share without forcing anyone else's hand. Bring whatever paperwork you have, even if it's incomplete; a probate gap or an old unrecorded assignment doesn't kill a deal, it just means the closing takes a little longer while title gets cleared.

Questions Owners Ask About This Topic

Does it matter whether the owner's Pecos County minerals are on the east or west side of the county?

Yes, considerably. Eastern acreage near the Reeves County line sits under active Wolfcamp and Bone Spring horizontal development, which tends to support a higher valuation than older vertical production farther west and south. the buyer team checks current permitting near your specific tract before quoting anything, rather than pricing off a county-wide average.

the owner's family has owned this interest since before the buyer team was born and the paperwork is thin. Can you still buy it?

Usually, yes. Pecos County has a long history of minerals passing through ranching-family inheritance, so incomplete records are common, not unusual. We work with a title company to fill gaps using probate and heirship records at the courthouse in Fort Stockton, and that process rarely stops a sale — it just adds time.

Is the owner's royalty check from Pecos County likely to keep growing or start declining?

Depends on where you sit. If your interest is tied to recent horizontal units on the eastern side, checks often ramp with new wells before settling into a decline curve typical of unconventional production. Legacy vertical wells on the western side tend to be past their growth phase and decline gradually. We factor your specific well history into any offer rather than guessing.

Do the buyer team has to sell everything, or can the buyer team sells part of the owner's Pecos County interest?

You can sell all of it, a percentage, or even just the rights on a single well or unit while keeping the rest. Some owners sell a portion to get cash now while keeping a smaller royalty stream for the long run. We'll price whatever slice you're comfortable parting with.

How long does closing usually take on a Pecos County mineral sale?

With clean title, often two to four weeks. If there's a probate gap, an heirship issue, or an old unrecorded deed to sort out, it can run longer while the title company clears it. We tell you upfront which situation you're in before you commit to anything.
